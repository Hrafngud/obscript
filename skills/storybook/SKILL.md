## Think in visual explanations

Give each scene one principal visual idea: a visual sentence that can be understood with the narration muted. Translate the spoken idea into visible cause and effect, transformation, scale, sequence, hierarchy, tension, comparison, or interaction. Do not re-explain the narration, justify the visuals, or turn its sentences into labeled boxes. The scene may focus on one component, one evolving infographic, one animated schematic, or one concrete technical action for its entire duration when that is the clearest explanation; do not add panels merely to fill the frame.

Plan each script section as a visual argument before dividing it into scenes. Reuse and evolve important objects, environments, and spatial relationships across adjacent scenes when the narration develops one idea; do not discard the visual world at every sentence. A scene boundary may reframe or advance the same coherent explanation instead of resetting to a new layout.

For a technical concept, choose the visual form that exposes its mechanism rather than the one that accommodates the most text. Suitable forms include:

- a literal workflow in a terminal, IDE, filesystem, browser, device, laboratory, or other real context;
- a schematic showing data, control, state, dependency, transformation, bottleneck, or failure moving through a system;
- a focused macro-to-micro or inside-the-system view that reveals otherwise invisible behavior;
- a concrete scenario or accurate visual analogy that preserves the concept's important limits;
- a diagram that changes over time as the explanation changes, instead of presenting a finished wall of facts.

When the script explains a technical system, data, computation, architecture, process, measurement, or abstract relationship, read and apply [the technical visual language](references/technical-visual-language.md). Select abstractions by semantics: directed arrows for real direction, traveling bright dots or tokens for discrete data or events, pulses for propagation, trees for hierarchy or recursion, node-link graphs for topology and dependencies, pipelines for ordered transformations, lanes for concurrency, queues for waiting or backpressure, layers for abstraction, state machines for control logic, diagnostic views for investigation, and the appropriate chart or graph for each quantitative claim. These are explanatory primitives, not decorations; keep their visual meaning consistent and do not imply unsupported flow, causality, scale, timing, certainty, or precision.

Show the operation whenever it can be shown: type the command, open the file, trace the request, transform the data, trigger the state change, expose the failure, or compare the observed result. Use brief labels only to orient the viewer. A definition, taxonomy, or collection of attributes is not by itself a visual explanation.

Keep required `narrative_beat` and `visual_goal` metadata to short phrases. Use `composition` for positions, scale, layering, depth, and meaningful relationships; `visual_elements` for concrete objects and their visible roles; and `animation` for timed actions on those objects. Specify "Hard cut" for a hard cut or "None" for inactive behaviors. The last `transition_out` is "None".

## Compose for focus and variety

Default to a dominant subject with a clear focal path, not a collection of peer cards. Containers, panels, windows, pills, and grids are appropriate only when literal interface structure, grouping, containment, or comparison is part of the meaning. Do not put an illustration inside a decorative card by habit. Let an illustration, schematic, environment, or interface occupy and organize the frame when it carries the explanation.

Do not default to two items side by side. Use the whole frame through depth, foreground/background interaction, guided paths, staged replacement, nested zooms, camera travel, full-frame transformation, progressive construction, or spatial cause and effect. Use split screens and side-by-side layouts when simultaneous comparison is genuinely the point. Review adjacent scenes and avoid repeating the same composition, entrance pattern, or text-plus-icon arrangement unless continuity makes the repetition meaningful.

Use charts and graph-like views whenever quantities, proportions, distributions, trends, relationships, or uncertainty are the explanation. Choose among bars, dots, line curves, step charts, areas, pie or donut charts, histograms, scatterplots, heatmaps, Sankey flows, treemaps, distributions, interval bands, and small multiples according to the question and the data. Animate the comparison or change being discussed, keep scales and denominators honest, and never use charts as generic visual texture.

Consider HyperFrames-supported perspective and 3D tilt when depth improves the explanation or composition: layered systems, isometric infrastructure, stacked planes, exploded assemblies, spatial networks, tilted interfaces, coordinate systems, and transitions between overview and detail. Use perspective, parallax, occlusion, and camera movement to clarify spatial relationships. Return dense text, code, legends, axes, and exact values to a readable front-facing view; never distort quantitative marks or add a permanent decorative tilt without semantic purpose.

Every illustration and asset must perform a semantic job: embody an actor or object, establish the setting or scale, demonstrate an action, reveal a relationship, carry attention, or become the transition. Animate meaningful parts of it or let the camera inspect it when useful. Do not use illustrations as corner ornaments, mascots, or empty-space fillers. If removing an element would not weaken comprehension, mood, continuity, or emphasis, omit it.

Motion should express the logic of the idea, not merely make static boxes enter. Preserve objects across beats when the viewer should track them; transform, route, accumulate, collide, reveal, or reframe them as the concept develops. Use motivated transitions by following, matching, or transforming a meaningful element into the next scene when appropriate. Playful timing, visual surprise, exaggeration, or characterful reactions may reinforce the script's wit, tension, relief, or wonder, but must remain accurate and purposeful.

The readable storybook displays `render_brief` as the scene's Layout description. Write it as a concise, self-contained English paragraph of imperative production directions. Lead with the focal visual and the explanatory action, then include the visible elements and asset references, their positions, scale, depth, and relationships, exact on-screen labels, background treatment, and the animation sequence from entrance to exit. Give scene-local seconds or relative timing such as "halfway through the scene" for changes of focus, fades, loops, and camera moves. Describe movement direction, what the camera follows, and what visible state changes. Include the transition to the next scene within the allocated interval. Prefer concrete actions such as "move the request token through the gateway; turn it red when the gateway rejects it" to vague effects such as "add a nice animation". Include only actions useful to this scene; a static shot needs no decorative motion.

Use supplied asset paths when available and name them consistently across `visual_elements`, `asset_requirements`, and `render_brief`. Do not claim a file exists if no input identifies it. For an asset that still needs sourcing or creation, describe it concretely in `asset_requirements` and refer to it by the same name in the directions. All visible elements, text, motion cues, and required assets in the structured fields must agree with the paragraph; the renderer should not have to invent missing scene decisions.
