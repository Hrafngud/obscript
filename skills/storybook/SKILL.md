## Build every scene on all five pillars

Treat the following as one indivisible scene contract, not a menu or a final checklist. Resolve all five before writing `composition`, `visual_elements`, `animation`, or `render_brief`. A scene is invalid when even one pillar is absent, generic, delegated to production, or contradicted by another field.

1. **Context correctness:** isolate the exact claim, relationship, action, or emotional turn in the scene's narration. Decide what visible evidence makes it true and what misleading implication the design must avoid. Record those decisions in `design_pillars.context`; every later visual decision must honor them.
2. **Assets:** run a scene-specific text search over `/home/zalmo/documents/obsidian/Videos/Videos/Globals/assets` using terms derived from the concrete subject, action, setting, visual primitive, and any named technology. Record the actual queries, verified candidate paths, chosen paths, usage, and semantic roles in `design_pillars.assets`. Search for every scene, even when adjacent scenes reuse an asset. At least `ceil(4 * total scene count / 5)` scenes must use a relevant foreground library asset. A background does not count as foreground coverage. An asset-free exception is allowed only after a real search finds nothing suitable, must state why the scene is clearer without a library asset, and may occupy no more than one fifth of scenes.
3. **Animation:** define a meaningful visible change and a guided attention path in `design_pillars.animation`, then realize both in the structured `animation` fields and `render_brief`. Every scene must transform state, demonstrate behavior, reveal a relationship, or move the viewer through an explanation. Entrances, exits, ambient loops, parallax, or camera drift alone do not satisfy this pillar.
4. **Visual abstraction:** select the semantic primitive that best matches the concept from [the technical visual language](references/technical-visual-language.md), even when the scene also contains literal environments, interfaces, illustrations, or characters. Record the primitive and the exact mapping between its marks or motion and the real concept in `design_pillars.visual_abstraction`, then preserve that mapping in the scene. Do not use an abstraction as decorative texture.
5. **Directness:** choose one dominant attention anchor—an object performing an action, a visible state change, a route, comparison, transformation, or consequence—and state what a viewer understands from it with narration muted in `design_pillars.directness`. Labels may orient the viewer but cannot carry the core explanation. If the scene becomes a title plus prose, a definition card, or narrated bullet list when its labels are removed, redesign it.

These fields are concise evidence of resolved design decisions, not prose rationales. They must describe the same scene as `composition`, `visual_elements`, `animation`, `asset_requirements`, and `render_brief`. Never satisfy one pillar by weakening another: a relevant asset still needs semantic animation; an accurate abstraction still needs a focal anchor; an engaging transition never excuses context drift.

Before authoring scenes, read [the complete good-scene example](references/good-scene-example.md). Use it as the quality bar for five-pillar integration and cross-field consistency, never as a template to copy. Choose a different composition, abstraction, motion sequence, and asset set whenever the narration calls for them.

Each scene belongs to exactly one script_section_id and contains an exact contiguous excerpt of that section's narration. Preserve punctuation and every word. Whitespace may vary. In script section order, concatenating the scene excerpts with whitespace must equal each section's entire narration. Cover every section exactly once in order, with no omissions, additions, duplication, or interleaving. Never reconstruct narration from script.md or rewrite it for timing.

Use IDs scene-001, scene-002, ... and order 1, 2, ... without gaps. Begin at zero, make adjacent estimated timing boundaries identical, and end exactly at target_duration_seconds. voiceover.estimated_seconds equals the scene's timing interval. Prefer scenes of 3–12 seconds, allowing exceptions for natural narration boundaries; distribute time according to narration density. Place boundaries at meaningful visual or narrative turns, not automatically at every sentence.

Production creates silent animations. A human reads the script and handles all audio separately. voiceover.text is the exact reference passage for that human, not a TTS request. Scene intervals become the animation timeline and script recording cues. Specify no audio assets, TTS, music, sound effects, or automatic subtitles; on-screen labels remain supporting visuals.

## Think in visual explanations

Give each scene one principal visual idea: a visual sentence that can be understood with the narration muted. Translate the spoken idea into visible cause and effect, transformation, scale, sequence, hierarchy, tension, comparison, or interaction. Do not re-explain the narration, justify the visuals, or turn its sentences into labeled boxes. The scene may focus on one component, one evolving infographic, one animated schematic, or one concrete technical action for its entire duration when that is the clearest explanation; do not add panels merely to fill the frame.

Plan each script section as a visual argument before dividing it into scenes. Reuse and evolve important objects, environments, and spatial relationships across adjacent scenes when the narration develops one idea; do not discard the visual world at every sentence. A scene boundary may reframe or advance the same coherent explanation instead of resetting to a new layout.

For a technical concept, choose the visual form that exposes its mechanism rather than the one that accommodates the most text. Suitable forms include:

- a literal workflow in a terminal, IDE, filesystem, browser, device, laboratory, or other real context;
- a schematic showing data, control, state, dependency, transformation, bottleneck, or failure moving through a system;
- a focused macro-to-micro or inside-the-system view that reveals otherwise invisible behavior;
- a concrete scenario or accurate visual analogy that preserves the concept's important limits;
- a diagram that changes over time as the explanation changes, instead of presenting a finished wall of facts.

Read and apply [the technical visual language](references/technical-visual-language.md) for every scene. Select abstractions by semantics: directed arrows for real direction, traveling bright dots or tokens for discrete data or events, pulses for propagation, trees for hierarchy or recursion, node-link graphs for topology and dependencies, pipelines for ordered transformations, lanes for concurrency, queues for waiting or backpressure, layers for abstraction, state machines for control logic, diagnostic views for investigation, and the appropriate chart or graph for each quantitative claim. For a literal or nontechnical scene, select the smallest semantic primitive that exposes its relationship, change, sequence, contrast, boundary, or consequence instead of forcing a technical-looking diagram. These are explanatory primitives, not decorations; keep their visual meaning consistent and do not imply unsupported flow, causality, scale, timing, certainty, or precision.

Show the operation whenever it can be shown: type the command, open the file, trace the request, transform the data, trigger the state change, expose the failure, or compare the observed result. Use brief labels only to orient the viewer. A definition, taxonomy, or collection of attributes is not by itself a visual explanation.

Keep required `narrative_beat` and `visual_goal` metadata to short phrases. Use `composition` for positions, scale, layering, depth, and meaningful relationships; `visual_elements` for concrete objects and their visible roles; and `animation` for timed actions on those objects. Specify "Hard cut" for a hard cut or "None" for inactive behaviors. The last `transition_out` is "None".

## Compose for focus and variety

Default to a dominant subject with a clear focal path, not a collection of peer cards. Containers, panels, windows, pills, and grids are appropriate only when literal interface structure, grouping, containment, or comparison is part of the meaning. Do not put an illustration inside a decorative card by habit. Let an illustration, schematic, environment, or interface occupy and organize the frame when it carries the explanation.

Do not default to two items side by side. Use the whole frame through depth, foreground/background interaction, guided paths, staged replacement, nested zooms, camera travel, full-frame transformation, progressive construction, or spatial cause and effect. Use split screens and side-by-side layouts when simultaneous comparison is genuinely the point. Review adjacent scenes and avoid repeating the same composition, entrance pattern, or text-plus-icon arrangement unless continuity makes the repetition meaningful.

Use charts and graph-like views whenever quantities, proportions, distributions, trends, relationships, or uncertainty are the explanation. Choose among bars, dots, line curves, step charts, areas, pie or donut charts, histograms, scatterplots, heatmaps, Sankey flows, treemaps, distributions, interval bands, and small multiples according to the question and the data. Animate the comparison or change being discussed, keep scales and denominators honest, and never use charts as generic visual texture.

Consider HyperFrames-supported perspective and 3D tilt when depth improves the explanation or composition: layered systems, isometric infrastructure, stacked planes, exploded assemblies, spatial networks, tilted interfaces, coordinate systems, and transitions between overview and detail. Use perspective, parallax, occlusion, and camera movement to clarify spatial relationships. Return dense text, code, legends, axes, and exact values to a readable front-facing view; never distort quantitative marks or add a permanent decorative tilt without semantic purpose.

Every illustration and asset must perform a semantic job: embody an actor or object, establish the setting or scale, demonstrate an action, reveal a relationship, carry attention, or become the transition. Animate meaningful parts of it or let the camera inspect it when useful. Do not use illustrations as corner ornaments, mascots, or empty-space fillers. If removing an element would not weaken comprehension, mood, continuity, or emphasis, omit it.

Motion should express the logic of the idea, not merely make static boxes enter. Preserve objects across beats when the viewer should track them; transform, route, accumulate, collide, reveal, or reframe them as the concept develops. Use motivated transitions by following, matching, or transforming a meaningful element into the next scene when appropriate. Playful timing, visual surprise, exaggeration, or characterful reactions may reinforce the script's wit, tension, relief, or wonder, but must remain accurate and purposeful.

The readable storybook displays `render_brief` as the scene's Layout description. Write it as a concise, self-contained English paragraph of imperative production directions. Lead with the focal visual and the explanatory action, then include the visible elements and asset references, their positions, scale, depth, and relationships, exact on-screen labels, background treatment, and the animation sequence from entrance to exit. Give scene-local seconds or relative timing such as "halfway through the scene" for changes of focus, fades, loops, and camera moves. Describe movement direction, what the camera follows, and what visible state changes. Include the transition to the next scene within the allocated interval. Prefer concrete actions such as "move the request token through the gateway; turn it red when the gateway rejects it" to vague effects such as "add a nice animation". Include only actions useful to this scene. Every scene needs explanatory motion, but never add decorative movement merely to keep the frame busy.

Use supplied and search-verified asset paths when available and name them consistently across `design_pillars.assets`, `visual_elements`, `asset_requirements`, and `render_brief`. Every selected path must appear among the recorded candidates and in the production fields. Do not claim a file exists if it was not verified. For an asset that still needs sourcing or creation, describe it concretely in `asset_requirements` and refer to it by the same name in the directions; this does not replace the required local-library search or justify exceeding the asset-free exception allowance. All visible elements, text, motion cues, and required assets in the structured fields must agree with the paragraph; the renderer should not have to invent missing scene decisions.

Use existing SVG icons, illustrations, technical symbols, emojis, raster backgrounds, and textures from `/home/zalmo/documents/obsidian/Videos/Videos/Globals/assets` wherever they fit the scene and shared direction. The library includes general-purpose icon collections, `tech-icons`, `tech2-icons`, `tech3-icons`, `emojis-svg`, and `background1`. Start from the visual explanation, then search by concrete nouns, actions, synonyms, technology names, interface types, and abstraction terms that could enact it; never distort the explanation to showcase an available asset. Inspect candidate filenames and the selected files read-only before recording them. Plan meaningful interactions around suitable library assets instead of requesting newly invented SVGs, hand-drawn abstract substitutes, generated images, or external stock. If a specific file cannot be verified, do not invent a path or count it as selected. Specify palette-appropriate recoloring for monochrome foreground icons and purposeful animation of icons, SVG parts, or emoji interactions. Preserve explicitly supplied assets and meaningful brand or emoji colors.

Use a custom raster image from `assets/background1` as a visible scene background in at least `ceil(total scene count / 5)` scenes, with a minimum of one background-image scene whenever the storybook has scenes. Distribute these scenes through the timeline instead of clustering them. A background counts toward this minimum only when the raster asset replaces the plain base as the full-frame background or occupies a substantial background region; a tiny icon, corner accent, or nearly invisible overlay does not count. Name the verified file path in `visual_elements`, `asset_requirements`, and `render_brief`, and give production an explicit placement treatment. More scenes may use custom backgrounds when they support the composition.

For raster background assets, inspect intrinsic dimensions, aspect ratio, and visible content relative to the output canvas. Use sufficiently resolved images as full-frame backgrounds with aspect-preserving cropping; treat tiny raster assets as likely texture tiles or substantial patterned regions instead of enlarging them across the screen. Specify repetition and tile scale, or placement across a large panel or masked region, in `composition` and `render_brief`. Check whether repeated edges form a usable pattern. Raster backgrounds and textures may be rotated, tilted, offset, cropped, or layered to build the composition; specify coverage beyond the frame before clipping transformed layers so no unintended gaps appear. Keep foreground text and icons legible. If dimensions cannot be verified during planning, state that production must inspect them before resolving scale and repetition.

Never generate, hand-author, request, or use an SVG as a scene background, even when an SVG file exists inside a background collection. Do not describe CSS-drawn or inline-SVG background substitutes. SVG icons and illustrations remain allowed as foreground elements. Before returning the storybook, count the qualifying raster-background scenes and confirm internally that the minimum above is met.

Supplement speech with on-screen keywords, numbers, labels, brief quotations, section markers, or short conceptual statements with density appropriate to the layout and reading time. There is no fixed word-count cap, but text must earn its place by orienting, identifying, contrasting, measuring, or delivering a necessary exact phrase. Do not introduce unsupported facts, automatically transcribe narration on screen, or use text to compensate for a visual that does not explain the idea.

Before returning the storybook, audit the five-pillar contract scene by scene. Reject and redesign any scene whose context evidence is ambiguous, asset search is absent, selected asset is passive or unrelated, motion is entrance-only, abstraction lacks a semantic mapping, or attention anchor depends on prose. Confirm that every selected path occurs consistently in all required scene fields, at least four fifths of scenes use a contextual foreground asset, and every asset-free exception is specific and necessary. Then review the sequence as a visual experience: confirm the focal visual communicates the intended relationship or change, illustrations have semantic roles, and the explanation would lose something if its main action were removed. Across the timeline, look for repeated card grids, habitual side-by-side layouts, passive icons, and sequences of entrance-only animation; redesign those patterns around the concept unless the repetition is meaningful. Prefer a smaller number of well-staged elements over comprehensive visual inventory.

Also review the sequence for technical-view variety and continuity. When supported by the material, alternate overview, operation, inspection, consequence, comparison, and synthesis while retaining the same visual ontology. Confirm that comparisons share an honest scale and framing, uncertain claims show their uncertainty, color is not the only carrier of meaning, and complex diagrams are progressively disclosed rather than rescued by excessive labels or legends.

If deterministic validation feedback is provided, regenerate the complete storybook and correct all reported defects. This remains a read-only stage: do not invoke HyperFrames or generate media. Return JSON only.
