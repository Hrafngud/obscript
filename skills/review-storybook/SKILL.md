name: review-storybook
description: Independently evaluate how well each obscript scene visually explains its narration, and find useful existing diagrams for weak representations. Use after storybook planning, before rendering.
---

Evaluate the supplied scenes as graphical explanations, independently of the writer's five-pillar claims. Return the supplied review schema, one concise finding per scene in the supplied order. Do not rewrite narration, timing, or the storybook. Directions and findings are in English; quote original-language labels accurately.

For each scene, identify the precise concept being spoken at that moment. Mentally play its actual visible actions with labels and narration removed. Does it expose the relevant mechanism, relationship, sequence, scale, contrast, or consequence? An accurate icon, polished entrance, decorative movement, or definition card can still be a shallow explanation. Compare the chosen representation with plausible alternatives from the supplied visual-language reference: an operation, changing schematic, route, state machine, queue, layered view, graph, chart, spatial analogy, or concrete demonstration. Choose for semantic fit, not maximal complexity or novelty. Evaluate the scene's directions, not its assertions that it is clear or explanatory.

Use `revise` for a material mismatch, misleading metaphor, passive decoration, missing explanatory action, or a clearly more informative graphical form. State the missing relationship and prescribe a concrete representation and timed visible action that production can execute. Do not fail an effective simple explanation for subjective taste. Use `pass` when the proposed action already communicates the right concept; keep optional suggestions brief. Review continuity with supplied neighbors without redesigning passing scenes.

When research is enabled, search for existing explanations of the weakest concepts, grouping scenes about the same concept. Budget: at most three search queries and six page/figure opens per batch, at most two useful references per scene. Prefer original technical documentation, authors' explanatory blog posts, and original papers (including arXiv). Inspect the actual diagram or figure, not just search snippets. Record the source page, exact direct image URL when available, title, attribution, observed license, and the specific mapping or animation worth adapting. Never invent a URL, inspection, permission, or license. If tools or relevant results are unavailable, report that honestly and still propose a concrete visual repair. Do not search merely for decorative stock images. Stop once a useful representation is found; reuse it across related scenes.

Distinguish `reference` (learn the explanatory structure and build an original scene), `reuse` (display the actual diagram with guided reveals), and `animate` (animate its actual geometry or use masks, highlights, traveling tokens, overlays, and camera focus on a raster diagram). Preserve the source's labels, scales, uncertainty, and causal meaning. Select reuse/animate only with an explicit reusable license or recorded permission; otherwise use reference. Merely appearing on arXiv or a public blog does not establish reuse permission. A source figure is not automatically better: prefer it only if its specific explanation improves this scene and is legible within its interval. Do not propose unlayering a raster into editable objects as if those layers already existed.

On the repair check, research is disabled. Judge only whether each flagged scene's revised visible action fixes the original problem, using the saved findings and diagrams. Do not introduce unrelated preferences or expand the research. Return JSON only; never render, generate media, download files, or edit inputs.
